From your experience, how do parents in Thailand think about heat and thermal comfort when choosing or using products such as prams, cots, or other baby equipment?

Dan

Parents don't really ask about heat. At a booth they ask about weight, how small it folds, whether it fits in the car, and the price. But you can see them thinking about it in other ways. They check the mesh panels, look for "ระบายอากาศ" on the tag, see how big the canopy is, and touch the fabric to feel if it's hot. Mostly they deal with heat by choosing where to go, not what to buy. They shop in malls, go out in the evening, and walk from the car straight into aircon. Cots hardly come up at all, because the baby sleeps in a room with the aircon on. There the parents worry the room is too cold instead.

Lovely, then what problems or unmet needs do you currently see around keeping babies comfortable or safe in hot conditions?

Dan

The main problem is that parents doesnt really have a way to know how hot the baby really is. They just put a hand on the back of the neck. I think a few things they miss: is how hot a car seat gets after an hour parked in Bangkok, strollers sitting low down where the road is hottest, and carrying the baby in a sling at an outdoor market.

What would you say makes a feature commercially attractive in this market?

Dan

Price bracket comes first. The market is split between premium at the top and very cheap at the bottom, with not much in between. So we ask whether the feature pushes the product into a bracket where we can still win. Next, can we show it in thirty seconds? Most of our sales come from fair booths and live selling, so if it needs explaining, it won't move.

Can you describe how customers in Thailand respond to baby products that include monitoring, sensing, alerts, or connected technology?

Dan

It goes both ways. Anything that calms a worried parent sells well, like cameras or anything that sounds like it prevents something bad. First-time parents especially. Connected products are harder. The app has to be in Thai, the setup has to work with a normal home router, and when something breaks the customer comes back to us, not the brand. People don't like subscriptions, and they don't trust things that send data somewhere.

If a new technology could help identify or manage heat exposure for babies using prams or cots, what would it need to offer for your company to seriously consider adopting or selling it?

Dan

Honestly, it has to be simple. If a parent needs to charge it or pair it with something, that's already one step too many. The reading should make sense the moment you look at it, without anyone explaining what the number means. Price matters a lot too. It can't push the product out of the range we already sell in, especially once discounts come in. And we need to be able to show it working at a booth in under a minute, otherwise it just won't move. I'd also keep the message about comfort rather than health, so we stay away from regulation issues. The supplier would need to cover warranty and repairs, because that all lands on us otherwise. And it should go into a line we already carry. If all of that lines up, I think the smarter move is building it into a stroller or car seat we already sell, instead of selling it on its own.
